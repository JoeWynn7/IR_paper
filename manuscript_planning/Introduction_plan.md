# Introduction plan (version 2, 25 November 2026)

This is a plan only; it contains no draft text. It replaces version 1, which is in the git history (commit `0d4d1c0`). The current draft in the manuscript (commit `8e0c289`) stays in place until a new draft based on this plan is agreed.

## 1. The argument in one line

Under public reputations, the problem of cooperation reduces to defining goodness. Under private, noisy assessment, defining goodness is only part of the problem; the other part is the agreed question: **how should reputations change in response to new assessments?** Our answer is asymmetric reputational inertia with partial consequences.

| ¶ | Role (frame A) | What the reader must take away | Words |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opening | The field asked how goodness should be defined; under public reputations, that question is the whole problem | 140–150 |
| 2 | Problem | Private, noisy assessment makes disagreements spread, most leading-eight norms fail, and the field turned to avoiding disagreements | 140–160 |
| 3 | Gap and question | Nearly all models fix how a reputation changes after an assessment, real reputations have inertia, and so the question arises | 150–170 |
| 4 | Approach | A reputation changes in two directions; misjudgments push both ways, so inertia may need to differ by direction: asymmetric reputational inertia | 170–190 |
| 5 | Message | Where inertia is placed matters; a single negative assessment should be costly but not catastrophic; goodness and reputation change must fit together | 160–180 |

Total: about 800–850 words, five paragraphs. Four paragraphs would force ¶4 and ¶5 into one block of more than 350 words.

## 2. Writing principles taken from top-journal introductions

1. **Ground the topic in observed behavior before naming the theory.** Tham, Hilbe & Murase 2026 (PNAS) and Murase & Hilbe 2024 (PNAS) open with what people do, then name indirect reciprocity.
2. **State modelling assumptions openly and credit what they made possible.** Murase & Hilbe 2024 call the public-assessment assumption extreme but successful because it makes analysis tractable.
3. **Give each mechanism one causal sentence that names both cause and effect.** Hilbe et al. 2018 explain the spread of disagreement this way. Avoid short fragments that only label a phenomenon.
4. **Build the gap by contrasting a specific assumption with real life, so that the question follows from the contrast.** Michel-Mata et al. 2024 (Nature) use this move for single-observation reputations.
5. **In the closing paragraph, say what is studied and what is found, without methods.** Hilbe et al. 2018, Tham et al. 2026 and Kawakatsu et al. 2024 all do this.
6. **Keep the register plain and confident.**
   - Present tense; active voice.
   - Sentences of mostly 15–30 words.
   - No intensifiers and no rhetorical labels.
   - At most one "however" per paragraph.

## 3. Paragraph plans

### ¶1 — From reputation to the classic question and its assumption

**Job.** Establish why reputations matter, introduce the classic question and its best-known answer, and end on the assumption behind that answer. The last two moves are the hinge for the whole Introduction.

**Moves, in order**

- **1.1 Opening claim.** Reputations link past behavior to future cooperation.
  - The subject is reputations.
  - Builds on your earlier opening.
  - No qualifier about strangers who never meet again.
- **1.2 Empirical grounding.** People help those who have helped others, and they cooperate more when their actions are observed.
- **1.3 Mechanism, named inside the sentence that explains it.** Indirect reciprocity: helping earns a good reputation, and a good reputation attracts help from third parties. Do not give the name a sentence of its own.
- **1.4 Why norms matter.** Whether an action counts as good depends on the norm that observers apply, and the same action can be judged differently under different norms. State this in general terms, with no worked example.
- **1.5 The classic question: how goodness should be defined.**
  - Tie it explicitly to Ohtsuki and Iwasa's question.
  - Phrase it so that it can later be mirrored by our question ("how should …").
- **1.6 The best-known answer.** The leading-eight norms.
  - Present them as the field's best-known answer or reference point.
  - Name their key property: stable cooperation even with occasional errors.
  - Avoid the "identified eight norms, the leading eight" construction.
- **1.7 The assumption.** The classic results assume public reputations: all individuals agree on everyone's reputation. Present this candidly as a simplification that made the analysis possible, not as an error.
- **1.8 The implication (hinge).** Under that assumption, the problem of cooperation reduces to finding the right definition of goodness. No citation; this is the sentence ¶3 and ¶5 come back to.

**How to write it**
- 1.7 and 1.8 are the strongest sentences in the paragraph: short and declarative, without hedges.
- Nothing about private information, misjudgments or inertia yet.

**Citations**
- 1.1–1.3: nowak1998, nowak2005, wedekind2000, milinski2002, milinski2016.
- 1.5: ohtsuki2004, santos2018, kessinger2023, murase2024computational.
- 1.6: ohtsuki2004, ohtsuki2006.

**Hand-off.** ¶2 removes the assumption stated in 1.7.

### ¶2 — When reputations are private

**Job.** Explain where disagreements come from, why they spread, what this does to the leading-eight norms, and how the field has responded. The paragraph ends on what existing mechanisms act on, which ¶3 needs.

**Moves, in order**

- **2.1 Topic sentence.** In real communities, reputations are private. Contrast directly with 1.7.
- **2.2 Sources of disagreement.**
  - Individuals see different interactions.
  - Noise in perception and assessment can make observers read the same interaction differently.
- **2.3 Propagation (the core sentence).** An observer judges each action through its own view of the recipient. A disagreement about one person therefore becomes a disagreement about the people who interact with that person, and disagreements multiply.
  - Name the cause and the effect in the same sentence.
  - This replaces both "these disagreements spread" and the unclear "extends to the donor".
- **2.4 Consequence.** Even rare errors erode the agreement that cooperation needs, and most leading-eight norms cease to be stable under private, noisy assessment.
  - Keep "most": Fujimoto & Ohtsuki (2023) find Simple Standing can be stable when everyone observes every interaction.
- **2.5 The field's response, framed as a shift.** Research has moved from how goodness should be defined to how disagreements can be avoided. Credit Tham et al. 2026, who make this observation.
- **2.6 Mechanisms, grouped by what they act on.**
  - Aligning observers' views: public institutions, gossip, the synchronization of opinions, dual assessment.
  - Changing how actions are judged: empathy, generosity.
  - Because 2.5 frames the list, it no longer opens abruptly the way "Proposed solutions include" did.

**How to write it**
- Use causal sentences rather than fragments.
- Do not name the punishment dilemma here. Wrongful condemnation is introduced in ¶4, where the two kinds of misjudgment are needed.
- End on what the mechanisms act on (what observers know, how actions are judged), because ¶3 turns on what they leave unchanged.

**Citations**
- 2.2: takahashi2006, uchida2010, hilbe2018.
- 2.3: hilbe2018, radzvilavicius2019, fujimoto2022.
- 2.4: murase2024synchronization; hilbe2018, okada2018, perret2021, fujimoto2023.
- 2.5: tham2026.
- 2.6: radzvilavicius2021, kawakatsu2024, murase2024synchronization, tham2026; radzvilavicius2019, schmid2021, okada2024.

### ¶3 — What nearly all models keep fixed: how reputations change

**Job.** Identify the element held fixed in both the classic theory and most work on disagreement, show why it matters however often misjudgments occur, contrast it with real reputations, note the models that relax it, and arrive at the question.

This paragraph is where a reviewer might ask: "if existing work already reduces errors, why study how errors affect reputations?" Moves 3.1–3.2 are built to answer that question in advance.

**Moves, in order**

- **3.1 The turn.** Name the fixed element as a specific modelling assumption, with citations: each new assessment replaces the observer's previous view of the actor.
  - Open assertively on this assumption.
  - Do not open with a comparison ("Like the classic theory").
- **3.2 Why it matters, whatever the frequency of misjudgments.**
  - Under this rule, a single assessment, even an erroneous one, can overturn a reputation however long it took to build. Every misjudgment that does occur, however rare, becomes a change of reputation and feeds into later judgments.
  - Make explicit that every reputation system needs some rule for turning assessments into reputations. That rule is part of the problem, not an alternative to reducing misjudgments.
- **3.3 Contrast with real life.**
  - Real reputations are not this fragile; they have inertia.
  - Use one short illustration covering both directions: a single lapse versus a single good deed.
  - Follow it with evidence: people revise impressions incrementally, and communities that govern shared resources start with mild sanctions that escalate only with repeated violations. This also prepares partial consequences in ¶4 and ¶5.
- **3.4 Models that relax the assumption (one sentence).** Models in which reputations keep more than the latest assessment can sustain cooperation where single-assessment models predict its collapse:
  - quantitative scores;
  - records of past defections;
  - aggregation of several observations;
  - abstaining from judgment when the recipient is in bad standing (the Staying norm, as one item in the list, not discussed separately).
- **3.5 The central point.** Defining goodness is only part of the problem of cooperation (refers back to 1.8).
- **3.6 The question.** State the agreed question in its exact wording as the other part of the problem.
  - It should follow from 3.5 without a label such as "complements the classic one".
  - The parallel with 1.5 ("how should …") is enough to link the two questions.

**How to write it**
- 3.1, 3.5 and 3.6 carry the paper; keep them crisp.
- 3.2 should sound like part of the argument, not a defence against a reviewer.
- No "Theoretical studies point in the same direction" and no "more robust".
- Describe earlier models by what they achieve, not by labels that could be disputed.

**Citations**
- 3.1: ohtsuki2004, hilbe2018.
- 3.3: mendesiedlecki2013neural, siegel2018, yamamoto2025; ostrom1990, cox2010.
- 3.4: schmid2023, clark2020, michelmata2024, sasaki2017, okada2018.

### ¶4 — From inertia to asymmetric reputational inertia

**Job.** Take the step from "reputations have inertia" to "inertia can differ between the two directions of change", explain why the direction matters in indirect reciprocity, and introduce the approach with a minimal model description.

**Moves, in order**

- **4.1 Opening (conceptual, not empirical).** A reputation can change in two directions, losing good standing and regaining it, and its inertia need not be the same in both.
  - This follows directly from the question in 3.6.
  - It replaces the unexplained empirical opening "This inertia often differs …".
- **4.2 Human evidence that the asymmetry exists, and that its direction varies.**
  - Trust is lost more easily than it is built.
  - Beliefs about people who have behaved badly are revised more readily than beliefs about people who have behaved well.
  - One sentence for each finding.
- **4.3 Why direction matters in indirect reciprocity: misjudgments push reputations both ways.**
  - Wrongful condemnation: a justified refusal to help is condemned by an observer who disagrees about the recipient.
  - Wrongful excuse: a norm that approves of refusing help to bad individuals makes an unconditional defector look justified.
- **4.4 The logic, in at most two sentences.**
  - Slowing the loss of good standing absorbs wrongful condemnations; slowing recovery absorbs wrongful excuses.
  - Norms differ in which kind they mainly produce, so the helpful direction may depend on the norm.
- **4.5 Approach.** Introduce asymmetric reputational inertia, using the title's wording, with a reference to Fig. 1. Losing and regaining good standing can require different amounts of evidence.
- **4.6 Model, in one or two sentences.**
  - A reputation changes only after a run of contrary assessments, which moves it through intermediate states.
  - The two paths can differ in length.
  - Intermediate states can already have partial consequences for treatment.
  - No symbols; no "depth" or "strength".

**How to write it**
- No candidate answer and no extended groundwork.
- Keep the misjudgment logic (4.3–4.4) to three sentences at most.
- The paragraph should read as motivation followed by the approach, not as results.

**Citations**
- 4.2: slovic1993, siegel2018 (optionally skowronski1989, mendesiedlecki2013diagnostic).
- 4.3: okada2020dilemmas.

### ¶5 — What we find, and why it matters

**Job.** Give the answer to the question in plain language and leave one memorable principle. No methods, numbers or symbols.

**Moves, in order**

- **5.1 Scope.** The four classic norms, named: Scoring, Shunning, Stern Judging and Simple Standing (ohtsuki2004).
- **5.2 Headline.** Where inertia is placed matters more than how much there is.
- **5.3 The rule.** Inertia helps when it protects the standing a norm most often overturns by mistake:
  - good standing under Scoring and Shunning;
  - bad standing under Simple Standing;
  - both under Stern Judging.
- **5.4 Effect on reputations.** With a small number of intermediate states placed this way, observers distinguish cooperators from defectors and largely agree with one another.
- **5.5 Effect on cooperation.** Cooperation becomes stable and is reached from most initial conditions, including under Shunning and Stern Judging, which fail without inertia.
- **5.6 Contrast.** The same amount of inertia in the opposite direction can eliminate cooperation.
- **5.7 Cost and principle.**
  - When isolated negative assessments have no consequences, individuals who defect only occasionally can invade.
  - They are excluded when a single negative assessment is costly but not catastrophic: it lowers a reputation without reversing it.
- **5.8 Closing message.** Cooperation requires both a definition of goodness and a way of changing reputations that fits it. This refers back to 1.8 and 3.5.

**How to write it**
- One sentence per finding, headline first.
- The final sentence should be the most memorable in the Introduction.
- Do not mention mean-field theory or simulations; these belong in the Methods.

## 6. Claims that need care

1. **"Each new assessment replaces the previous view."** True for the classic models, including third-order norms. The exceptions are exactly the models cited in 3.4.
2. **"Most leading-eight norms fail."** Keep "most", because of Fujimoto & Ohtsuki 2023.
3. **Earlier models (3.4).** Describe what they show. "Look twice, forgive once" has the same reputation map as our simplest tolerant record, so the Introduction must not claim that our model differs from aggregation in kind. Our contribution is:
   - asymmetry across directions;
   - partial consequences;
   - dependence on the norm.
4. **Human asymmetry (4.2).** Say that its direction varies.
5. **Frame B** (consistent private reputations without communication) stays in the Discussion. No sentence in the Introduction should argue it.
6. **Yamamoto et al. 2025** has been checked against the abstract only.
