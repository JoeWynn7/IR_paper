# Introduction plan

**Guiding question:** *How should reputations change in response to new assessments?*

**The gap behind it:** the rule that turns assessments into reputations is fixed by convention (the latest assessment replaces the previous view). Under private information, this rule decides whether misjudgments are absorbed or amplified.

**Format:** five paragraphs, about 850–900 words for NHB. For PNAS, the same structure can be cut to about 700 words.

## 1. The line of argument

Each paragraph supplies the premise for the next.

1. Reputations are built from assessments of actions, and theory has asked how behavior should be evaluated. A reputation is not an assessment, but models equate the two by convention. (¶1)
2. Under private information, assessments contain misjudgments of two kinds. Each one immediately changes a reputation, so misjudgments spread and cooperation fails. (¶2)
3. Most proposals reduce how often misjudgments occur. Their damage also depends on how strongly each one changes a reputation. People, institutions and recent models let reputations change gradually. This raises the question. (¶3)
4. Changing only when evidence accumulates gives reputations inertia, and inertia protects deserved and undeserved standing alike. The two directions absorb different misjudgments, and norms differ in which misjudgment prevails. So the two directions may need different inertia: asymmetric reputational inertia. (¶4)
5. The answer: where inertia is placed matters more than how much there is. Tolerance has a cost that partial consequences remove. Cooperation depends on how reputations change. (¶5)

| ¶ | Frame A role | Story role | Reader's question on entering | What the reader takes away | Words |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opening | Setting | What is this about? | There are two questions, and models settle the second by convention | 150–170 |
| 2 | Problem | Complication | Why does the convention matter? | Under private information it lets misjudgments spread | 170–190 |
| 3 | Gap | Turn and question | What else can be done besides reducing misjudgments? | How should reputations change in response to new assessments? | 150–170 |
| 4 | Approach | Insight and model | What would a good answer look like? | Inertia cuts both ways, so we model asymmetric inertia | 200–220 |
| 5 | Message | Resolution | What is the answer? | Direction matters; partial consequences; the message | 140–160 |

Editors often read only the first and last paragraphs. ¶1 must carry the general importance and the distinction, and ¶5 must carry the answer and the message. Both are written to stand alone.

## 2. Paragraph plans

Beats are numbered (1.1, 1.2, …) so that comments can refer to them. "Anchor" marks proposed wording for the sentences that carry the logic.

### ¶1 — Setting: the classic question and the convention

**Job.** Introduce reputations and the field's classic question. Separate assessing an action from changing a reputation. Show that models settle the second matter by convention.

**The reader takes away.** There are two questions. Theory has studied the first. Models answer the second with a convention.

**Hands to ¶2.** The phrase "this convention".

**Beats**

- **1.1 Hook.** Reputations allow cooperation among individuals who may never meet again. Cite `nowak2005, milinski2016`.
  - Make "reputations" the grammatical subject: it is the protagonist of the paper.
- **1.2 Mechanism.** Helping earns a good reputation, and a good reputation attracts help from third parties. Name the mechanism: indirect reciprocity. Cite `nowak1998, wedekind2000, milinski2002`.
- **1.3 Roles.** In each interaction, a donor decides whether to help a recipient, and observers assess the donor's action according to a social norm.
  - Defines donor, recipient and observer once, so ¶2 can use them without explanation.
- **1.4 Classic question.** Much of the theory has asked how behavior should be evaluated. Cite `ohtsuki2004, santos2018, kessinger2023, murase2024computational`.
- **1.5 Concrete instance.** Whether refusing to help an individual with a bad reputation should be judged good or bad. Cite `panchanathan2003, ohtsuki2004`.
  - Chosen on purpose: both misjudgments in ¶2 arise in exactly this situation.
- **1.6 Achievement.** The leading eight sustain cooperation when all individuals share the same view of each other's reputations, even when errors occur. Cite `ohtsuki2004, ohtsuki2006`.
  - "Share the same view" prepares the contrast with private reputations in ¶2.
- **1.7 Distinction (pivot).** No citation.
  - Anchor: "An assessment concerns a single action, whereas a reputation is an observer's view of an individual."
- **1.8 Convention (pivot).** Cite `ohtsuki2004, hilbe2018`.
  - Anchor: "In most models, the question of how a reputation should change is settled by convention: the latest assessment replaces the observer's previous view."

**How to write it**

- Present tense. One idea per sentence, 15–25 words each.
- 1.7 and 1.8 carry the paper. Keep them short, plain and next to each other.
- 1.8 describes; it does not criticize. "Settled by convention" names the question without claiming that it was neglected, and it sets up "reopen" in ¶3.
- Use *assess/assessment* for single actions. Use *evaluate* only in "how behavior should be evaluated".
- Do not mention private information, misjudgments or inertia yet.

**Accuracy notes**

- With third-order norms, the assessment can depend on the donor's previous reputation. The new view is still the latest assessment, so "replaces" remains correct.

**Reader test.** Can the reader state both questions, and the conventional answer to the second?

### ¶2 — Complication: the convention fails under private information

**Job.** Show that, under private information, the convention lets every misjudgment change a reputation, so misjudgments spread and cooperation fails. The paragraph also introduces:

- the two misjudgments and their dependence on the norm, which are premises for ¶4–5;
- the two properties that reputations lose, which are used in ¶5.

**The reader takes away.** Misjudgments of two kinds are unavoidable. Under the convention, each one changes a reputation immediately, so they spread.

**Enters from** "this convention" (1.8). **Hands to ¶3:** cooperation fails, so what has been done about it?

**Beats**

- **2.1 Topic sentence.** "When reputations are private, this convention makes them fragile."
- **2.2 Why views differ.** Observers witness different interactions, and their assessments are subject to errors. Cite `takahashi2006, uchida2010, hilbe2018`.
- **2.3 Why assessments differ.** An observer assesses an action in light of its own view of the recipient, so different observers can assess the same action differently.
- **2.4 Wrongful condemnation.** A donor that refuses to help a recipient it regards as bad is condemned by an observer who regards that recipient as good. Cite `okada2020dilemmas, michelmata2024`.
- **2.5 Wrongful excuse.** Under norms that approve of refusing help to bad individuals, an unconditional defector is excused whenever the observer regards its recipient as bad.
- **2.6 Norm dependence.** Which kind prevails depends on how a norm assesses actions towards recipients regarded as bad.
  - One short sentence. ¶4 refers back to it.
- **2.7 Role of the convention (pivot).** Because the latest assessment replaces the previous view, each misjudgment changes a reputation at once. The changed reputation then affects how later actions towards that individual are assessed.
- **2.8 Spread.** Misjudgments therefore spread through the population. Cite `hilbe2018, radzvilavicius2019, fujimoto2022`.
- **2.9 Two properties lost.** Reputations become inaccurate, failing to distinguish cooperators from defectors, and inconsistent, with observers disagreeing about who is good. Cite `michelmata2024, murase2024synchronization`.
  - The words "accurate" and "consistent" return unchanged in ¶5.
- **2.10 Outcome.** Most leading-eight norms no longer sustain cooperation. Cite `hilbe2018, okada2018, perret2021, fujimoto2023`.

**How to write it**

- Write 2.4 and 2.5 in the same form: "Cooperators can be wrongly condemned: … Defectors can be wrongly excused: …". The two labels are reused in ¶4.
- Keep 2.7. It makes the paragraph about the convention rather than a general summary of private assessment.
- Write "assessments are subject to errors", not "err".
- Order: where disagreement comes from → misjudgments → role of the convention → spread → consequences. The topic sentence states the conclusion, and 2.7 gives the reason.

**Accuracy notes**

- The convention does not create disagreement. It makes each misjudgment fully effective, and 2.7 says exactly this.
- Keep "most" in 2.10:
  - Fujimoto & Ohtsuki (2023) find that Simple Standing can be stable when every individual observes every interaction.
  - Okada et al. (2018) find that its cooperative state shifts to coexistence with unconditional cooperators.
- The excuse in 2.5 is a misjudgment of the defector's strategy, not a misapplication of the norm. The Results should say this once.

**Reader test.** Can the reader name both misjudgments and explain why they spread?

### ¶3 — Turn and question

**Job.** Position current proposals, which reduce how often misjudgments occur. Turn to the second factor: how strongly each misjudgment changes a reputation. Show that people, institutions and recent models let reputations change gradually. State the question.

**The reader takes away.** The question, and how it differs from the classic one.

**Enters from:** cooperation fails (2.10). **Hands to ¶4:** the question.

**Beats**

- **3.1 Current proposals.** Most mechanisms proposed to restore cooperation under private assessment reduce how often misjudgments occur.
- **3.2 Examples.**
  - Institutions, gossip and the synchronization of opinions align the views of different observers. Cite `radzvilavicius2021, kawakatsu2024, murase2024synchronization, tham2026`.
  - Empathetic or generous assessment avoids some condemnations. Cite `radzvilavicius2019, schmid2021, okada2024`.
- **3.3 Turn (pivot).** Anchor: "The damage caused by a misjudgment, however, also depends on how strongly it changes a reputation."
- **3.4 People.** Reputations seldom change on the basis of a single observation. People revise impressions gradually, and they revise good and bad impressions at different rates. Cite `skowronski1989, mendesiedlecki2013diagnostic, siegel2018, yamamoto2025`.
  - Plants the asymmetry.
- **3.5 Institutions.** Communities that manage shared resources respond to a first violation with a mild sanction and escalate only after repeated violations. Cite `ostrom1990, cox2010`.
  - Plants the partial consequences.
- **3.6 Models.** Cooperation under noisy or private information becomes more robust when reputations accumulate evidence. Examples:
  - quantitative scores, `schmid2023`;
  - records of past defections, `clark2020`;
  - aggregation of several observations, `michelmata2024`;
  - optionally, the Staying norm, which leaves a donor's reputation unchanged when its recipient is regarded as bad, `sasaki2017`.
- **3.7 Question (pivot).** Anchor: "These findings reopen a question that complements the classic question of how behavior should be evaluated: how should reputations change in response to new assessments?"

**How to write it**

- 3.1–3.2 only position the work, in neutral terms. Do not add that these mechanisms require shared information: that contrast belongs to frame B and the Discussion.
- 3.3 is short and contains "however". It is the hinge of the Introduction.
- Order the evidence from familiar to formal: people → institutions → models.
- 3.6 says what the models show, not how they are built. Avoid labels such as "symmetric" or "tolerant", which can be disputed for individual models.
- 3.7 states the agreed question word for word. "Reopen" answers "settled by convention" in 1.8.

**Accuracy notes**

- 3.4: the direction of the asymmetry differs between studies. Negativity bias points one way; the volatility of beliefs about bad agents points the other. "At different rates" is accurate without naming a direction.
- Yamamoto et al. (2025) was checked against the abstract only.
- "Most" in 3.1 is correct, because the exceptions are exactly the models cited in 3.6.

**Reader test.** Can the reader state the question, and say how it differs from the classic one?

### ¶4 — Insight and approach

**Job.**

- Take the answer suggested by ¶3: change only when evidence accumulates.
- Show that it creates inertia, and that inertia cuts both ways.
- Derive that the two directions may need different inertia.
- Introduce the model, the norms and the methods.

**The reader takes away.** Why direction could matter, and what the model allows.

**Enters from:** the question (3.7). **Hands to ¶5:** a defined model and four norms. The reader now expects to learn where inertia should be placed.

**Beats**

- **4.1 Candidate answer.** These findings suggest that a reputation should change only when evidence against it accumulates, which gives it inertia.
- **4.2 Complication (pivot).** Anchor: "Inertia, however, protects deserved and undeserved standing alike."
- **4.3 Slowing the loss of good standing** protects a cooperator from a single wrongful condemnation. But it also protects a defector whose good reputation stems from a wrongful excuse.
- **4.4 Slowing the recovery of good standing** prevents a single wrongful excuse from restoring a defector's reputation. But it also delays the recovery of a wrongly condemned cooperator.
- **4.5 Implication.** Because the prevailing misjudgment depends on the norm (refers back to 2.6), the inertia that helps may differ between losing and regaining good standing.
- **4.6 Approach.** "Here we study indirect reciprocity with asymmetric reputational inertia (Fig. 1)."
- **4.7 Core rule.** An established reputation changes only after a run of contrary assessments. A single assessment that supports it ends the run.
  - The second sentence separates the model from scores. Recommended.
- **4.8 Warning states.** Negative assessments move an individual in good standing through warning states before its reputation turns bad.
- **4.9 Recovery states.** Positive assessments move an individual in bad standing through recovery states before its reputation turns good.
- **4.10 Asymmetry.** The two sequences can differ in length, so losing and regaining good standing can require different amounts of evidence.
- **4.11 Partial consequences.** While evidence accumulates, intermediate states can already have partial consequences for how an individual is treated.
  - Optional link back to 3.5: "as a mild first sanction does".
- **4.12 Scope and methods.** Four classic norms that differ in which misjudgment they produce: Scoring, Shunning, Stern Judging and Simple Standing. Cite `ohtsuki2004`. Methods: a mean-field theory of private reputations and individual-based simulations.

**How to write it**

- 4.3 and 4.4 must be strictly parallel ("Slowing X …, but it also …"). This pair is the intellectual core of the Introduction: it is what makes the asymmetry intuitive.
- "Asymmetric reputational inertia" appears once, in 4.6, matching the title.
- Use plain words only. No k₁, k₂, α or β, and no "depth" or "strength"; these are defined in the Results.
- 4.5 says "may differ". The finding itself belongs to ¶5.
- Here, partial consequences are a feature of the model, not a second question. Their role appears in ¶5.

**Accuracy notes**

- Reviewers may ask how warning and recovery states differ from scores or ternary reputations. The answer goes in the Results and Discussion: runs that are reset by supporting evidence, separate lengths, partial consequences. Do not address it in the Introduction.
- If ¶4 feels heavy, move 4.12 to the start of ¶5 ("Analysing four classic norms …, we find …").

**Reader test.** Can the reader explain why the direction of inertia could matter?

### ¶5 — Resolution and message

**Job.** Answer the question as a principle, and close the loop opened in ¶1.

**The reader takes away.** Reputations should change with inertia that protects the standing a norm most often overturns by mistake. Incomplete evidence should already have partial consequences.

**Beats** (with the Results each one previews)

- **5.1 Headline.** "We find that where inertia is placed matters more than how much inertia there is." (Fig. 4)
- **5.2 Rule.** Inertia helps when it protects the standing a norm most often overturns by mistake: good standing under Scoring and Shunning, bad standing under Simple Standing, and both under Stern Judging. (Figs. 2 and 4)
- **5.3 Reputations.** Placed this way, inertia makes private reputations accurate and consistent. (Fig. 2; refers back to 2.9)
- **5.4 Cooperation.** Cooperation becomes evolutionarily stable and is reached from most initial conditions under all four norms. This includes Shunning and Stern Judging, which do not sustain cooperation without inertia. (Fig. 3)
- **5.5 Wrong direction.** The same amount of inertia placed in the opposite direction can prevent cooperation altogether. (Fig. 4)
- **5.6 Cost.** Because inertia tolerates isolated negative assessments, individuals who defect only occasionally can invade. (Fig. 5a)
- **5.7 Resolution.** Such individuals are excluded when warning states carry partial consequences, so that a single negative assessment lowers a reputation without reversing it. (Fig. 5b and Extended Data Fig. 8; refers back to 3.5 and 4.11)
- **5.8 Message (pivot).** Anchor: "Cooperation therefore depends not only on how behavior is evaluated but also on how reputations change."

**How to write it**

- Use "We find that" once; state the rest plainly.
- No numbers, symbols or figure references.
- Order: headline → rule → reputations → cooperation → wrong direction → cost → resolution → message. The headline comes first because it is the answer; the rest follows the order of the Results.
- 5.8 mirrors ¶1 and the question. Avoid "as much as" and "crucial".

**Accuracy notes**

- 5.2: under Simple Standing, the best architectures combine a short warning path with a longer recovery path. "Bad standing" summarizes the dominant direction.
- 5.4: under Simple Standing, the cooperative state is a mixture of discriminators and unconditional cooperators. "Cooperation becomes stable" covers this.
- 5.5 holds under Scoring, Shunning and Stern Judging, where inertia on recovery alone gives no cooperation.
- 5.7 holds only within a bounded range. "Partial" signals that consequences that are too strong also fail.

**Reader test.** Could the reader answer the question in one sentence?

## 7. Style rules for drafting

- No more than about 30 words per sentence. One idea per sentence. No nested clauses.
- Paired ideas use identical syntax (2.4 and 2.5; 4.3 and 4.4).
- Each paragraph opens with a sentence that links to the end of the previous one.
- No parentheses except the figure reference. No symbols or abbreviations.
- No intensifiers or evaluative words: crucial, key, novel, remarkably, importantly, dramatically.
- At most one "however" per paragraph.
- Present tense for established results and for the model. "We find" for our results.
- Citations as `~\cite{}`, placed before the full stop, with two to four keys per claim.
